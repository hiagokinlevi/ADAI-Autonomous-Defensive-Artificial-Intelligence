Abstract
Defensive security automation is converging on a capability the field has not given a principled account of:
software agents that decide, unprompted, to change the configuration of production infrastructure in order
to stop an attack in progress. Existing work treats this either as orchestration (playbook engines, which
execute but do not decide) or as control (reinforcement learning in simulated networks, which decide but
do not act on production). Neither answers the question that determines deployability: under what
conditions is it acceptable for a machine to act on infrastructure with no human in the loop, and what must
it be able to prove afterwards?
We argue the answer is not a confidence threshold on a model's output but a structural property of the
system. We define the action envelope as a conjunction of four independently checkable predicates —
defended perimeter, bounded effect scope, reversibility with pre-declared rollback, and verification by a
capability-disjoint credential — adjudicated by a decision function that is pure in a declared action contract
and therefore provably independent of any learned component. We define the evidence obligation as a
hash-linked, seal-terminated transcript with a self-describing recomputation contract, and prove that
tamper localization is exact — recovering the altered index from the least failing index together with which
verification conjunct failed, a distinction a boolean verifier discards, misattributing half of all single-turn
alterations to the following, unaltered turn.
We identify and formalize two problems that we believe are new to the literature. The discriminator
condition states necessary and sufficient conditions under which per-principal containment is expressible
at a shared enforcement point, and shows that in the common case of multiple principals behind one
enforcement domain, source-address predicates alone cannot achieve a blast radius of one. The
aggregation–reconciliation tension shows that the rule-budget pressure which forces effects to be
aggregated destroys the bijection between effect and rule that reconciliation algorithms assume, changing
reconciliation from identifier-keyed lookup in O(R) to element-wise existence testing in O(A); we give the
corrected algorithm, and show that the same aggregation makes the rule shared mutable state, admitting a
lost update that the write reports as success and that only independent verification detects.
We give a five-level autonomy lattice ordered by what may change without a human rather than by model
quality, an evaluation methodology separating three families of measurement whose conflation we argue is
the field's central methodological error, and a characterization of the properties that admit no finite test
and must therefore be discharged by construction.

1. Introduction
1.1 Three literatures that do not meet
Security orchestration. Playbook engines map a signal to a fixed response sequence [17, 4]. Every branch
was authored by a person; when the signal matches no branch, nothing happens. Such systems are
accountable precisely because they are not autonomous, and their accountability does not transfer to
systems that are.
Autonomic computing. The Monitor–Analyse–Plan–Execute loop over a shared Knowledge base [18, 16]
gave the field its vocabulary for self-managing systems. It is a control architecture and is silent on the
question that dominates security: Plan may be wrong — adversarially so, since the input is partly authored
by an adversary — and Execute changes a system other parties depend on. The loop describes the stages; it
does not state what must hold before Execute may run.
Learned cyber defense. A substantial body of work trains agents, by reinforcement learning or by
prompting large language models, to defend simulated networks [37, 14, 25]. These results are informative
about policy quality and silent about deployment: simulators have no tenants, no credentials, no third
parties whose traffic an erroneous block interrupts, and no obligation to explain afterwards.
The gap lies between the second and the third: an architecture for agents that decide, deployed where
consequences are real.
1.2 ADAI: the class that falls in the gap
We name the class autonomous defensive artificial intelligence (ADAI) and define it by membership
criteria rather than by capability, so that the question "is this an ADAI?" has an answer a reader can check
rather than an answer its vendor asserts.
Definition 1.1 (ADAI). A system is an autonomous defensive artificial intelligence iff it satisfies all four of:

A. It acts on production infrastructure. It effects configuration changes to systems carrying real
traffic for real dependents — not to a simulator, not to a staging copy, and not by producing a
recommendation a person then enacts.
B. It decides without contemporaneous approval. For at least one class of action, the determination
to act is the system's, taken at the time of the incident, with no human in that loop. A system whose
every branch was authored in advance satisfies A and fails B.
C. Admissibility is adjudicated outside the component that proposes. The conditions under which
action is permitted are evaluated by a function whose domain excludes the output of any learned or
generative component, so that the permitted set is fixed before that component runs (§3).
D. It produces evidence a non-operator can verify. Each action yields a record whose integrity a
party holding none of the system's code, and no access to its storage, can check from the record alone
(§4).
Definition 1.2 (Degenerate cases). A system satisfying A and B but not C is unconstrained: its safety
properties are properties of a component the adversary partly authors. A system satisfying A, B and C but
not D is unaccountable: it may be safe and cannot demonstrate it, which is the same position as being
untrusted. A system satisfying C and D but not B is an audited orchestrator — a valuable thing, and not this
thing.

1.2.2 How to test membership
The criteria were chosen so that a reader can evaluate them without the vendor's cooperation on three of
the four.
1. A — observe whether a configuration change occurred on a system carrying real traffic. Directly
observable.
2. B — observe whether a human approved this instance. Ask for the approval record; absence of one for
an action that occurred settles it.
3. C — read the signature of the admissibility function. If the vendor will not show it, the criterion is
unsatisfied for the reader's purposes: an unexaminable claim is not a satisfied criterion. Where source is
unavailable, a weaker but still useful test is adversarial: submit signals whose free-text fields argue for
an inadmissible action and observe whether the permitted set moves.
4. D — take one record, implement a verifier from its published description, and run it. Then alter one
field and confirm the verifier rejects, and that the index it reports is the index altered (§4.3). This is the
strongest of the four tests and the cheapest to run.
A system failing test 4 is not an ADAI regardless of what its documentation says, and the test costs an
afternoon.
Two consequences of stating the class this way are worth drawing out.
The criteria are falsifiable, and C and D are falsifiable by inspection. One checks C by reading the
decision function's signature; one checks D by attempting the verification with none of the vendor's code
and observing whether it succeeds. Neither requires trusting a claim. A category whose membership could
only be asserted would not be worth naming.
Autonomy is not the achievement; C and D are. Criterion B is the easy one — any system can be made to
act unprompted, and most of the hazard in this field comes from systems that have done exactly that. What
makes the class coherent is that B is admitted only in the presence of C and D. An ADAI is therefore not "an
AI that defends by itself"; it is an AI permitted to defend by itself because the conditions of its acting are
fixed outside it and the record of its acting is checkable outside it.
The remainder of this paper gives the framework that discharges C and D. The companion paper describes
a deployed system satisfying all four.
1.3 Why model confidence is the wrong gate
The instinct is to gate autonomous action on the model's confidence. This is the wrong instrument for three
reasons, in increasing order of severity.
Calibration. Modern neural networks are poorly calibrated even in benign conditions [12, 27], and the
input to a defensive agent is partly authored by the adversary [2, 11]. Any confidence derived from that
input inherits the adversary's influence over it. Calibration guarantees obtained on benign distributions do
not transfer.
Wrong codomain. Confidence is a scalar over the space of propositions. What governs admissibility is the
space of consequences: how much is damaged if the proposition is false, and whether the damage is
reversible. A high-confidence decision to null-route a /16 is strictly worse than a low-confidence decision to
present a challenge to one address.

Non-persistence. A confidence threshold is a property of a component. Components are replaced,
retrained, and reprompted. A guarantee located in a model's output does not survive any of those events; a
guarantee located in the system's structure survives all three.
We therefore place the decision outside the learned component entirely, and the remainder of this paper
makes that placement precise.

3.4 Why each predicate is structural, not procedural
Each predicate is chosen so that it can be discharged by construction rather than by convention, because
convention fails silently.

1 is discharged by credential scoping and by binding the perimeter to the principal. The
characteristic failure it prevents is subtle: a system that correctly filters reads by tenant and does not filter

12. Conclusion: autonomous defense is possible, and this is how
The question this paper set out to answer was whether a machine may be permitted to defend
infrastructure on its own — deciding, at the moment of an incident, without a human in the loop, and
acting on systems that carry other people's traffic. We have argued that the answer is yes, that it is yes
under stated conditions rather than in general, and that the conditions are structural rather than a matter
of how good the reasoning component happens to be.
The results of §§3–11 assemble into a single constructive statement, and we give it in that form because a
framework of prohibitions read as a list of prohibitions misrepresents what it is for.

Read against the phases of incident handling [4], the theorem covers three of them and stops precisely
where the deployment's reach stops, which is worth stating rather than blurring.
Containment is direct. Guarantees (a)–(c) are exactly what is needed to permit a machine to change an

and it can be withdrawn. This is the phase the framework addresses most completely.
Eradication is bounded by the enforcement point's reach and we do not overstate it. A system acting
at a network edge removes an adversary's access, not its foothold: it ends the reach, and cannot clean a
compromised host. What guarantee (d) adds is the part eradication actually requires and that most
automation lacks — that the response be complete, with every effect accounted for. An adversary's access is
not eradicated by a block that some later process silently reverted and nobody noticed. The review loop

Why the reasoning component's fallibility is not fatal
The move that makes all of this work is a separation, and it is worth stating plainly because it is the paper's
central claim about how autonomy becomes admissible.
A defensive agent needs judgement: which signals matter, which target is the adversary, which response
fits. Judgement of that kind is the province of components that are fallible, that are trained on data, and
whose input is in part written by the adversary. Building a safety argument on such a component's accuracy
is building it on the adversary's cooperation.
So the system does not. The set of admissible actions is fixed by the catalogue and the envelope before any
reasoning runs, and is checkable without running it. The choice within that set is where judgement lives,
and where a learned component may contribute. A model that is wrong picks a permitted action that a
better model would not have picked; it does not pick an impermissible one, because the impermissible ones
are not in the set it is choosing from.
This is what makes the difference between an autonomous system and an unsupervised one. The first has a
boundary someone drew deliberately and can inspect; the second has whatever boundary its most fallible
component happens to produce. The failures in §9 are of two shapes, and both are silent for the same
reason: six where the guarantee lived in convention rather than in construction, and one where it lived in
construction but the construction implemented less than the guarantee stated. The second shape is the
harder one, and it is the reason §8.2 attacks the predicates instead of reading the code that implements
them — a boundary one can inspect is worth drawing only if inspection is something other than re-reading
one's own intention.
The claim, stated exactly
A machine can be permitted to prevent, contain and account for attacks on production infrastructure, on its
own, when: it acts only on evidence it extracted structurally and verified independently; the boundary of
what it may do was drawn before it ran and does not move; every effect it creates is bound to one
principal, reversible, and reachable from an evidential record; and that record can be checked by someone
who does not trust it.
Those conditions are not easy, and this paper's contribution is that they are stated — as theorems with
proofs, with the boundaries where each fails and an instruction to refuse the capability there rather than
claim it. A system meeting them fails loudly where it fails — fail-stop rather than fail-silent [34, 10] — and
that, not infallibility, is what a machine acting on other people's infrastructure owes the people who depend
on it.
